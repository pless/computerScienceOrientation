Emergent Behavior: The Game of Life and Boids

Some computer games have very simple rules, but they make complex patterns that are hard to predict. This is called emergent behavior. People started to study games like this more than 50 years ago.

Today you will explore two famous examples: Conway's Game of Life and Craig Reynolds' Boids. Both are easy to program. At the end, you will write a post on your blogs.gwu.edu blog about one of them.

Part 1: The Game of Life (must do)

The Game of Life is a discrete game. The world is a grid of cells, and time moves in steps. Each cell is “on” (alive) or “off” (dead). Each cell has 8 neighbors: the cells next to it, also on the diagonals.

At each step, every cell counts its “on” neighbors. Then all the cells change at the same time, with these rules:

The cell is now“On” neighborsAt the next step, the cell is
on2 or 3on (it stays alive)
on0 or 1off (too lonely)
on4 or moreoff (too crowded)
offexactly 3on (a new cell is born)
offany other numberoff

That is all! These rules make patterns that move, grow, or disappear.

Think first

Answer these questions in your head before you use the simulator.

  1. All the cells are off. What happens at the next step?
    Answer: All the cells stay off. No cell has 3 “on” neighbors, so no new cell is born.
  2. All the cells are on. What happens at the next step?
    Answer: Each cell in the middle of the board has 8 “on” neighbors. That is more than 3, so these cells turn off.
  3. Only one cell is on. What happens?
  4. Three cells in a row are on. What happens to each of the three cells? What happens to their neighbors?

Try it

Open the simulator: kylepaulsen.com/stuff/game_of_life.html.

  1. Click Randomize Board to fill the grid with random cells.
  2. Click Simulate to start. To move one step at a time, click Next Gen.
  3. To draw your own cells, click and drag in the box.

Spend 5 minutes on this. Look for these patterns:

  • Patterns that never change. These are stable.
  • Patterns that change, but repeat the same shapes again and again or move across the board. These are dynamically stable.

Part 2: Boids (must do)

Boids is a continuous game. There is no grid. Each boid moves smoothly, and its position can be any point on the screen. “Boid” is short for “bird-oid object”: a simple model of a bird.

Each boid obeys three rules. A slider controls each rule.

RuleWhat each boid doesWhat the slider controls
Coherence (also called cohesion) Flies toward the other boids near it. How quickly it turns toward them.
Separation Steers away so that it does not hit other boids. How close it gets to another boid before it turns away.
Alignment Tries to match the speed and direction of the boids near it. How closely it matches them.

Try it

Open one of these simulators:

  • eater.net/boids
  • boids.cubedhuang.com

Move the sliders and watch the boids. What happens when a slider is at its smallest value? What happens at its largest value?
